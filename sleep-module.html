Learning goals

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  1. Explain how evening screen light reaches the body clock and what it changes.
  2. Describe why emerging adults are especially exposed to this risk.
  3. Compare four evidence-based strategies, including barriers and who benefits most.
  4. Adapt advice so it respects culture, work and family responsibilities.

Why emerging adults?

Emerging adulthood is the stretch between adolescence and settled adult life (Arnett, 2007). Parents’ rules and school schedules fade, and young people must manage sleep, study and screens on their own (Roisman et al., 2004). Mental health vulnerability also rises: anxiety, depression and pre-sleep hyperarousal become more common.

Psychological drives

High socio-emotional responsiveness and desire for peer connectedness create vulnerability to digital telepressure and Fear of Missing Out (FOMO) (Steinberg, 2008). Social apps help young adults sustain close peer ties (Vaterlaus et al., 2016), which makes switching off feel costly.

Adolescent development pairs an early-maturing reward system with a slower-maturing cognitive control system (Steinberg, 2008); resisting hyper-stimulating, algorithm-driven media in unstructured evenings remains demanding in emerging adulthood.

The science: from screen to sleep

Screens emit blue-enriched light with a peak between 450 and 480 nm. This light is detected by special retinal cells that signal the brain’s master clock (Schöllhorn et al., 2023).

What evening screens do

Also affected: deep sleep (slow-wave sleep) is compressed, REM sleep is suppressed, the normal nightly drop in body temperature is dampened, and next-morning attention worsens (more omission errors on attention tests) (Green et al., 2017; Höhn et al., 2024).

Culture and context: one size does not fit all

“Emerging adulthood” was built on Western, middle-class assumptions of independence and self-focus. For many young people, adulthood means family responsibility, work, or caring for others, often with little time for prolonged exploration (Arnett, 2007; Katsiaficas et al., 2014).

Family first

Collectivist and immigrant-origin students may support household finances, translate or care for siblings (Katsiaficas et al., 2014).

Economic necessity

Low-income and first-generation students may take on full adult roles right after school (Katsiaficas et al., 2014).

Code-switching

Students move between campus self-reliance and home interdependence (Katsiaficas et al., 2014).

Societal norms

In Greek higher education, family capacities, norm compliance and multi-generational co-residence are emphasized over early independence (Tsipianitis & Karalis, 2019).

Practice tip: Avoid blanket rules like “turn off all devices at 9 PM.” Screens may be a student’s only internet access, a family lifeline or a stress-relief tool. Offer options that protect sleep while keeping access: spectral display tuning, a flexible wind-down window and bedroom changes that fit the space.

Building the skills

Information alone does not change behavior. Programs that include supervised skills practice are 5 to 7 times more likely to produce lasting change than lectures about sleep hygiene (Conley, 2015).

Five social-emotional skills applied to screens

Adapted from the CASEL competencies as used in higher education (Conley, 2015).

  • Self-awareness: notice pre-sleep arousal and FOMO triggers.
  • Self-management: relaxation, cognitive restructuring and limits on compulsive checking.
  • Social awareness: consider how late-night messages affect peers and partners.
  • Relationship skills: set healthy digital boundaries without losing connection.
  • Responsible decision-making: plan study time and a screen curfew.
What effective practice looks like
  • Demonstration and supervised instruction
  • In-session practice plus daily home practice logs
  • Reminders such as weekly text prompts
  • Delivery through first-year seminars, residence halls and counseling centers
  • Methods: mindfulness (MBSR), CBT techniques for bedtime procrastination, progressive muscle relaxation and breathing
How to measure progress
  • Body: DLMO, actigraphy, light sensors
  • Behavior and attention: sleep scales (PSQI, ESS, KSS, MISS), continuous performance tasks
  • Mood and media use: PHQ-4, CES-D, in-bed media logs
